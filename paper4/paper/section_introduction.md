## Introduction

A road operator who installs traffic sensors pays for each one, and covers only part of the network. Two
decisions follow:

- what each sensor should measure;
- how many sensors to install, and where.

For the second decision, the usual hope is that instrumented segments inform the segments without a sensor.
Spatio-temporal graph models propagate information along the road network [wu2019gwnet], and kriging methods
estimate unobserved locations from observed ones [wu2021ignnk]. If that propagation works, coverage and
placement buy accuracy on the whole network, not only where the sensors are.

We test that hope on one task: classifying congestion in three classes (free flow, slowed, congested) at
15–120 minutes ahead, on segments that have no sensor. A segment without a sensor is never seen by the model:
it is not in the input, in the training loss, in the normalisation or in the class weights. This is the
operator's situation, and it is stricter than the evaluation of a deliverable that preceded this work, where
the segments said to be hidden were training targets.

The data are the measured speeds of three cities from the Traffic4cast benchmark (Madrid, London and Melbourne,
828, 774 and 587 segments) [ref: Traffic4cast 2022]. The forecast is scored by weighted cross-entropy, which we
abbreviate as wCE: the cross-entropy of the predicted class probabilities, with class weights that give the
rare congested class the same total weight as the others.

The reference is a model without spatial exchange. It is the same temporal network as the graph model, Graph
WaveNet [wu2019gwnet], with its graph removed. It forecasts a segment from the calendar it has learned, since
the segment's own data never reach it.

Against that reference we ask whether instrumented neighbours help, in four ways:

- with a learned graph model, varying the spacing of the network and the instrumented fraction;
- with predictors that need no training, which read the neighbours' labels directly;
- with those labels calibrated on the instrumented segments, with and without the calendar;
- by measuring how close each placement rule brings the uncovered segments to a sensor.

Every experiment was pre-registered before any test metric was computed, under the method of an earlier study
by the same authors [ref: Paper 1]: signed pre-registrations, frozen records generated by script, intervals
from a joint resample of seeds and blocks of the network, and two reading scales (the sign of the interval and
a margin of 3% of the reference wCE).

We address four research questions.

- **RQ1 (what to measure).** Against an independent speed reference, what does a loop detector measure of
  congestion: its level, its occurrence, or both? How much of the disagreement on urban arterials is due to the
  spatial matching of loops and road segments, and what does aggregating lanes hide?
- **RQ2 (which neighbours).** On segments without a sensor, does the geometry of the road graph matter for a
  graph model, against a randomised graph with the same degrees and weights, as the spacing of the network
  changes?
- **RQ3 (how many).** How does the forecast on segments without a sensor change with the instrumented fraction
  of the network, and is there a coverage above which propagation from instrumented neighbours pays?
- **RQ4 (where).** Does the rule that chooses which segments to instrument change the forecast on the others,
  and through what: is there a spatial signal in the labels that a placement could bring within reach?

The answers to RQ2–RQ4 are negative, and they are negative in a precise sense.

- The graph model gains nothing over the model without spatial exchange: not on compact sub-networks at any
  spacing from 43 to 397 m, and not on whole cities at any coverage from 10% to 60%. Where the graph adds
  nothing, which neighbours it connects cannot matter.
- The labels do carry a spatial signal. Within about 250 m, a segment without a sensor shares its nearest
  instrumented neighbour's class more often than chance. Beyond that distance the agreement is close to chance.
- That signal is not a forecasting resource. Calibrated on the instrumented segments, it comes within 1–3
  points of the reference inside 250 m and does not pass it.
- A table of class frequencies by hour of the week forecasts as well as the reference. Once that calendar is
  known, the neighbour's class adds nothing within 250 m and makes the forecast worse beyond.
- Placement does change proximity. At the same number of sensors, uniform placement brings six uncovered
  segments in seven within 250 m of a sensor in Madrid and London, against one in seven for placement in
  contiguous blocks. Given the previous points, that proximity buys no forecast.

Our thesis follows from these answers. **On segments without a sensor, these data carry no forecastable
spatial information beyond the calendar at 15–120 minutes. What an operator buys with a sensor is the
measurement at that segment, and the choice of what to measure.**

## Contributions

1. *An inductive evaluation of propagation to segments without a sensor.* Hidden segments never enter input,
   loss, normalisation or class weights. Node sets are drawn as whole blocks of the network, with three
   disjoint sets for training, checkpoint selection and evaluation. A positive control shows what the same
   pipeline reaches when hidden segments are training targets. In this regime, propagation gives no
   measurable gain over a model without spatial exchange, on sub-networks and on whole cities.
2. *A decomposition of the spatial signal without models.* We measure the agreement of nearby labels, then
   its value as a calibrated forecast, then its value net of the calendar. Each step uses the same segments,
   origins and resample as the trained models. The agreement is short, and its forecasting value is that of
   the shared weekly rhythm. This is why a further learned model has nothing to extract.
3. *A geometric account of placement.* For three rules at equal numbers of sensors, we report how many
   uncovered segments lie within the radius of the signal. Placement decides where the network measures, not
   how well it forecasts elsewhere.
4. **[Part A]** *What a loop detector measures.* To be stated once Part A is frozen.
5. *Reproducible artefacts.* We release the signed pre-registrations, the frozen records with the fingerprints
   of every input, the per-cell predictions, a register of corrections, and one script that regenerates every
   number of the paper.
